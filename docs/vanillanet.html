The one-sentence thesis

“More is different” built modern AI.
This paper asks: what if less is enough?

Foundation models grew by piling on depth and intricate operators. VanillaNet bets the opposite: a network so plain it’s almost trivial to deploy — and proves the plainness need not cost accuracy.

Keep one question in mind: if a layer adds no accuracy at inference, why pay for it at inference? VanillaNet’s answer is to use it while training and delete it before deployment.

Part 1 · Problem definition

Complexity is great for accuracy —
and terrible for deployment.

Every accuracy win of the last decade came bundled with an engineering tax that shows up at inference time:

🔀 Shortcuts cost memory traffic

ResNet’s skip connections merge features from different layers, consuming heavy off-chip memory bandwidth on real hardware.

🧩 Exotic ops need custom kernels

Shifted-window attention (Swin) and axial-shift (AS-MLP) require hand-written CUDA — fragile to port and deploy.

🏗️ Depth blocks parallelism

ViT-Base is ~62 layers; each must finish before the next starts. Sequential depth can’t be parallelised away.

🌫️ Plain nets were abandoned

Since ResNet, pure-conv nets fell out of fashion: a 34-layer plain net trained worse than an 18-layer one (gradient vanishing).

The gap: nobody was optimising simple, shallow networks anymore — even though they’re by far the easiest to ship. VanillaNet reopens that abandoned door.

Part 1 · The counter-intuitive insight

On a modern GPU, depth — not FLOPs — sets the speed.

With batch size 1 and abundant compute, a GPU parallelises within a layer effortlessly. What it can’t hide is the sequential chain of layers. So a “cheap” deep net can be slower than an “expensive” shallow one:

The reframing: if latency is bounded by depth, the winning move isn’t fewer FLOPs — it’s fewer layers. Spend FLOPs freely inside each layer; just don’t stack many.

Part 1 · Logical progression from prior work

A deliberate U-turn in the architecture story.

2012 · AlexNet
Shallow & plain wins ImageNet
12 plain layers beat everything. The starting point — simple, stackable conv blocks.
2014 · VGGNet
Go deeper, stay plain
Uniform 3×3 stacks. Depth helps… until plain nets stop training well.
2015 · ResNet — the fork
Shortcuts rescue depth
Skip connections fix gradient vanishing, so 50–1000 layers become trainable. But the field reads this as “plain nets are dead.”
2020–22 · ViT · Swin · ConvNeXt
Even deeper, even more intricate
Self-attention and elaborate blocks push accuracy higher — and deployment complexity with it.
2023 · VanillaNet
Revisit the fork — go shallow, not deep
Take the abandoned plain-net path, but instead of fighting gradient vanishing with depth, avoid depth entirely and fix the real bottleneck: weak non-linearity.
Part 3 · The architecture (VanillaNet-6)

Stem → body → head. One layer per stage.

The classic recipe — but stripped to the bone. Each stage is a single 1×1 conv + activation + max-pool. Channels double, resolution halves. No branches, no SE-blocks, no shortcuts (they tested — shortcuts didn’t help).

Why this is a deployment dream: every layer is a plain 1×1 conv — a single matrix multiply. No CUDA tricks, no merged branches, runs fast on literally any hardware.

Part 2 · The catch

Shallow + plain = weak non-linearity = weak accuracy.

A naive 6-layer plain ReLU net tops out around 60.5% on ImageNet — useless in practice. The bottleneck isn’t identity mapping (shortcuts don’t help). It’s that too few activation layers means too little non-linearity.

The whole paper is two ideas that inject non-linearity — without adding a single layer or operation to the deployed model:

🪜 Trick 1 · Deep Training

Train as a deeper net, then mathematically collapse the extra layers into the shallow one before inference. Capacity while learning, simplicity while serving.

train-time onlyzero inference cost

〰️ Trick 2 · Series Activation

Make each activation far more expressive by stacking it in parallel, so one activation layer does the work of many.

richer per-layer~1% of conv FLOPs
Part 2 · Key concept ① — Deep Training

Start deep with activations. Fade them to identity. Merge.

Each layer begins as two convs with an activation between them. The activation is blended toward the identity over training: A′(x) = (1−λ)·A(x) + λ·x with λ = e / E. Drag λ to watch it collapse:

The merge: two layers become one, exactly & for free

① Fold each BatchNorm into its preceding conv (scale γ, shift β, mean μ, var σ):

W′ᵢ = (γᵢ/σᵢ)·Wᵢ    B′ᵢ = (Bᵢ−μᵢ)·γᵢ/σᵢ + βᵢ

② With the activation gone (λ=1), the two stacked 1×1 convs are pure matrix multiplies. A 1×1 conv’s im2col is just a reshape, so:

y = W¹ ∗ (W² ∗ x) = (W¹·W²) ∗ x  ⟶ one conv

The product W¹·W² is precomputed once. Inference sees a single conv — the second layer cost vanishes entirely, with bit-exact output.

Part 2 · Key concept ② — Series-Informed Activation

Stack activations in parallel, not in series.

Deep nets gain non-linearity by stacking activations serially (= more depth = more latency). VanillaNet stacks them concurrently inside one layer: a learnable weighted sum of shifted ReLUs that approximates a far richer curve. Drag n:

n=0 is plain ReLU. Each added term is a scaled, shifted ReLU — together they bend into smooth, expressive shapes.

As(x) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ aᵢ·A(x + bᵢ)

aᵢ, bᵢ are learned per channel. Extended to also mix spatial neighbours (like BNET), so one activation gathers global context.

Ablation: n=0 → 60.5% · n=1 → 74.5% · n=3 → 76.4%. One idea recovers nearly all the lost accuracy.

Why it’s almost free: the complexity ratio

A 1×1 conv costs O = H·W·Cᵢₙ·Cₒᵤₜ·k². The series activation costs only O = H·W·Cᵢₙ·n². Their ratio:

O(CONV) / O(SA) = (Cₒᵤₜ·k²) / n²  ≈ 2048·1 / 7² ≈ 84×

At stage 4 of VanillaNet-B (Cₒᵤₜ=2048, k=1, n=7) the activation is ~84× cheaper than the conv it sits next to. Expressivity for almost no FLOPs.

Part 3 · What does it actually learn?

A wide, holistic gaze — its strength and its limit.

Grad-CAM++ on VanillaNet-9 vs ResNet-50 reveals a different style of seeing:

🟢 Strength · broad coverage

VanillaNet activates over a much larger region. With many channels and FLOPs per layer, it extracts all relevant signal across the image rather than a narrow patch.

🟡 Limit · weaker isolation

Its lower non-linearity makes it less able to focus on a single discriminative part — the honest trade-off of going shallow.

Interpretation: depth buys the ability to carve out fine, part-specific attention. VanillaNet compensates with width and brute coverage — good enough to match deep nets on classification, by a different mechanism.

Part 3 · The implementation, end to end

Two models in one codebase.

The released implementation cleanly separates the training graph from the deployment graph:

🏋️ Training mode

Each block = Conv → BN → A′(x) → Conv → BN, with series activation. λ scheduled 0 → 1 over the deep-training epochs. Strong regularisation (large per-layer capacity).

⚡ Deploy mode · switch_to_deploy()

Fold BN → conv, then fold the two convs into one. Activations are now identity, so they disappear. Result: a flat stack of single 1×1 convs.

Train deep

STEP 2

STEP 4

Wrap-up · The contribution, distilled

What VanillaNet really teaches.

⏱️ Latency ≠ FLOPs

When compute is abundant, depth is the cost. Optimise the metric that actually bottlenecks deployment.

🔀 Decouple train & infer

Capacity is only needed while learning. Collapse it away before serving — re-parameterisation as a design principle.

〰️ Non-linearity > depth

For shallow nets the real bottleneck is per-layer non-linearity, not identity mapping. Fix that, drop the shortcuts.

The minimalist thesis, proven: you can match ResNet and Swin with 6–13 branch-free layers — by moving every drop of complexity into training and deleting it before inference. Less, done cleverly, is enough.

Limitations worth raising in Q&A: high params/FLOPs per layer (memory-heavy), weaker part-level focus, strong-regularisation dependence, and classification-centric evaluation.
